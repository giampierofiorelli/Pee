# 7. Methodological knowledge, goals and evaluation

Requirements addressed: R14, R15.

## 7.1 Principle

Knowledge about **how to investigate and learn** is represented in the same universe, with the same apparatus as
knowledge about the world (T10). Methods are items. Contents about them have standing and grounds. They are revised
when evidence about their performance changes. This matches the view that aims, methods and theories adjust to one
another, with none of them fixed in advance (Laudan, *Science and Values*, 1984). It also matches error-statistical
methodology, where knowledge of methods consists largely of knowledge of how methods can fail and how those failures
can be detected (Mayo, 1996).

## 7.2 Categories of methodological knowledge

| Category | What it is | Representation |
|---|---|---|
| **Question** | a request for information with presuppositions and a space of possible answers. Kinds include what, whether, how much, why and how (Belnap and Steel, *The Logic of Questions and Answers*, 1976; Hintikka, *Inquiry as Inquiry*, 1999) | an item whose answer space is defined by concepts. Its presuppositions are contents with standing. A question with a false presupposition is defective, and this can be detected |
| **Goal** | a state of affairs an agent seeks | a content with an agent-relative conative attitude "desired" (sections 5.9, 7.4) |
| **Inquiry** | an occurrent: a goal-directed sequence of observations, inferences and decisions | participants: agents, targets, methods, questions |
| **Method** | a type of procedure (D16) | see section 7.3 |
| **Study design** | a method for producing evidence of a given kind: a randomised trial, a cohort study, a case–control study, a field survey, a natural experiment, a controlled laboratory experiment | a method with known validity conditions and known biases |
| **Instrument and protocol** | material and procedural means of observation | entities and methods with calibration and validity contents |
| **Standard and criterion** | a norm for judging adequacy: a threshold of evidence, a reporting standard, a tolerance | a methodological content with holder and purpose |
| **Heuristic** | a defeasible rule of thumb for generating or prioritising hypotheses ("look for a conserved quantity"; "try the simplest model first") | a method whose reliability profile is weak or unknown, marked as such |
| **Learning method** | a method for acquiring concepts, models or skills: M1–M6 (section 6.1), induction of models from data, analogy | a method like the others, applicable to the universe itself |

## 7.3 What a method's description contains

Any method in the universe can be described by:

1. **Purpose**: the kinds of question it answers or the kinds of content it produces.
2. **Applicability conditions**: the targets, data and circumstances for which it is designed.
3. **Assumptions**: what must hold for its output to be reliable. Examples are random sampling, no unmeasured
   confounding, calibration and measurement invariance.
4. **Inputs and outputs**: the kinds of item consumed and produced.
5. **Procedure**: the ordered steps, at the level of detail available. Some skills are tacit (O15).
6. **Warrant**: why the method works when its assumptions hold, as a content with grounds. Examples are a
   mathematical theorem, a calibration study or a track record.
7. **Failure modes**: known ways it goes wrong and how they can be detected. Examples are selection bias, overfitting,
   instrument drift and p-hacking.
8. **Reliability profile**: what is known about its error rates, sensitivity and specificity, with standing.
9. **Relations**: to the frames it presupposes, to methods it refines or competes with, and to the communities that
   use it.

Contents produced by a method carry the method in their grounds (section 5.9). **When the universe's view of a
method changes, every content that depends on it is flagged** (section 6.3, propagation). The replication crisis in
parts of psychology and medicine shows why this matters. A large replication project found that many published
effects did not replicate at the original strength (Open Science Collaboration, "Estimating the reproducibility of
psychological science", 2015). That finding is methodological knowledge, and it should change the standing of
contents grounded in the affected practices.

## 7.4 Goals, values and evaluation

The later adviser must understand human goals and evaluate products and interventions (R15). S0 therefore defines
the semantic categories needed, without building the adviser.

- **Goals** are contents held by an agent with the conative attitude *desired*, with a priority and with constraints.
  The attitude is conative, not epistemic (section 5.9). They are
  kept apart from contents the agent *accepts* as true. That a person wants a treatment to work is not evidence that
  it does.
- **Values and criteria** are explicit methodological or evaluative contents: safety, efficacy, cost, environmental
  impact, fairness. Which values are in force, and with which weights, is relative to a holder. It is never hidden
  inside factual contents. This follows the analysis of the legitimate and illegitimate roles of values in science
  (Douglas, *Science, Policy, and the Value-Free Ideal*, 2009).
- **Artefacts and interventions** are represented as entities and occurrents with **functions**: what they are
  designed or used to do (Houkes and Vermaas, 2010). They also have **performance quantities**, measured under
  stated conditions.
- **An evaluative content** (D10) has this form:

  ```
  ⟨ item X meets criterion K to level L, for goal G of holder H,
    under conditions C, compared with alternative A, on grounds Γ ⟩
  ```

  Every one of these parameters is mandatory. An evaluation that leaves out its conditions, its comparator or its
  holder is incomplete, and must be marked so.
- **Rated versus realised performance.** A product's performance under standard test conditions is a fit content of
  a test model. Its performance in a particular use is a transfer hypothesis (section 6.4) with its own grounds. The
  two must not be confused (example E8).

## 7.5 Methodological knowledge about the universe itself

The universe's own procedures are methods in this sense, and can be described and assessed with the same
apparatus. These include its acquisition modes (6.1), recognition (3.5), revision policy (6.3) and coherence checks
(6.5). Two limits apply:

- A sufficiently expressive language cannot define its own truth predicate without paradox (Tarski, 1936; conditions in section 10.2, item 5). So the
  universe's assessments of itself must be **stratified**: a content about the reliability of a method is
  evaluated by other methods, not by itself alone. The exact form of the stratification is a choice for S1 (O13).
- Agreement among the universe's own methods, or among copies of the same system, does not count as independent
  evidence (section 5.8, independence).
